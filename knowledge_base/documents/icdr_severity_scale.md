# International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) Disease Severity Scale
Source: Wilkinson et al., "Proposed International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy
and Diabetic Macular Edema Disease Severity Scales," Ophthalmology 2003;
110:1677-1682. (Content below is paraphrased for internal knowledge-base use,
not reproduced verbatim from the original publication.)

## Grading levels

**No apparent retinopathy (Grade 0):** No visible abnormalities on dilated
fundus exam.

**Mild NPDR (Grade 1):** Microaneurysms only. No other lesions present.

**Moderate NPDR (Grade 2):** More than just microaneurysms, but the eye does
not yet meet any of the "severe" criteria below. May include a modest number
of dot/blot hemorrhages, hard exudates, or cotton wool spots.

**Severe NPDR (Grade 3):** Defined by the **4-2-1 rule** -- an eye meeting ANY
ONE of the following three criteria qualifies as severe NPDR, provided there
are no signs of proliferative disease:
  - More than 20 intraretinal hemorrhages in EACH of the four retinal
    quadrants, OR
  - Definite venous beading in TWO OR MORE quadrants, OR
  - Prominent intraretinal microvascular abnormalities (IRMA) in ONE OR MORE
    quadrants.
An eye meeting two or more of these three criteria simultaneously is
sometimes referred to as "very severe" NPDR and carries an even higher
short-term risk of progression to PDR.

**Proliferative DR / PDR (Grade 4):** One or both of the following:
  - Neovascularization (new vessel growth on the disc, elsewhere in the
    retina, or in the iris/angle), OR
  - Vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage.
PDR is further subclassified as high-risk vs. non-high-risk based on the
location and extent of neovascularization and the presence of vitreous
hemorrhage -- high-risk PDR (e.g. NVD occupying roughly a quarter to a third
of a disc area, or NVD/NVE accompanied by vitreous or preretinal hemorrhage)
warrants urgent treatment.

## Diabetic Macular Edema (DME) is graded separately and can co-occur at any
severity level:
  - **No apparent DME:** No retinal thickening or hard exudates within one
    disc diameter of the fovea.
  - **Mild DME:** Retinal thickening/exudates present but distant from the
    macular center.
  - **Moderate/Severe DME:** Thickening or exudates approaching or involving
    the center of the macula -- this is the threshold most associated with
    risk to central vision and typically triggers anti-VEGF or laser
    consideration regardless of the NPDR/PDR severity grade.

## Clinical progression risk (approximate, pooled from published cohort
studies)
  - Mild NPDR: very low near-term risk of progression to PDR.
  - Moderate NPDR: roughly 20-25% cumulative incidence of PDR over ~4 years;
    faster progression reported in some Southeast Asian cohorts (median
    ~4-5 months to progress moderate -> severe). One large anti-VEGF-naive
    cohort reported 48-month cumulative PDR incidence of ~7.9% for mild
    NPDR, ~20.9% for moderate NPDR, and ~46.8% for severe NPDR at baseline
    -- illustrating a roughly 2.7x hazard increase moderate-vs-mild and a
    ~6.5x hazard increase severe-vs-mild.
  - Severe NPDR: substantially higher short-term risk -- published estimates
    are roughly 15-20% risk of high-risk PDR within 1 year and up to ~40%
    within 3 years.
  - Baseline severity is itself the strongest predictor of progression:
    the Wisconsin Epidemiologic Study of Diabetic Retinopathy (WESDR)
    found ~54% less 10-year progression to PDR in type 2 diabetic patients
    with no baseline retinopathy compared with those who already had
    severe NPDR at baseline.
  - Note: venous beading in 2+ quadrants, while part of the formal 4-2-1
    rule, has been reported in some populations to correlate more strongly
    with existing PDR than with imminent progression from severe NPDR --
    treat it as a strong severity marker rather than a precise progression
    timer.

## Diabetic Macular Edema (DME): OCT-based staging (modern, supersedes
pure clinical CSME grading in anti-VEGF-era practice)
Source: ETDRS (1985, original CSME definition); ICO Guidelines for
Diabetic Eye Care (2017); DRCR.net / RIDE-RISE trial literature.
Paraphrased summary.

  - **Clinically Significant Macular Edema (CSME)**, the original ETDRS
    definition based on biomicroscopy alone: retinal thickening at or
    within 500 micrometers of the macular center, OR hard exudates at or
    within 500 micrometers of the center if associated with adjacent
    retinal thickening, OR a zone of retinal thickening one disc area or
    larger, any part of which lies within one disc diameter of the
    macular center.
  - **OCT-based grading (current standard)**, per ICO 2017 guidance:
      - No DME: no retinal thickening or hard exudates in the macular
        region.
      - Non-center-involving DME: retinal thickening present but not
        involving the central 1 mm subfield.
      - Center-involving DME: retinal thickening involves the central
        1 mm subfield -- this is the threshold most consistently used to
        trigger anti-VEGF therapy in modern practice.
  - **Central Subfield Thickness (CST)** is the quantitative OCT metric
    most commonly used in trials: mean retinal thickness within a 1 mm
    circle centered on the fovea. A CST >= 275 micrometers (time-domain
    OCT convention, e.g. RISE/RIDE trials) or >= 275-320 micrometers
    (spectral-domain OCT, device-dependent) is commonly used as the
    "center-involving" / treatment-eligible threshold, though the exact
    cutoff remains debated and device-dependent -- CST should be
    interpreted relative to the specific OCT platform's normative range
    rather than as a single universal number.
  - DME can occur at ANY DR severity level, including mild NPDR, and its
    course is considered partially independent of the NPDR/PDR severity
    grade -- a system should grade DME status separately rather than
    folding it into the 5-level NPDR/PDR scale.
  - OCT morphological DME patterns (informational, not required for
    triage): sponge-like retinal swelling, cystoid macular edema (most
    common pattern in some cohorts), subretinal fluid, and posterior
    hyaloid traction -- traction patterns may warrant surgical rather than
    injection-based management.
