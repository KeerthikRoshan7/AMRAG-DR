# Treatment Protocols for Diabetic Retinopathy and Diabetic Macular Edema
Source: Synthesized from AAO PPP treatment guidance and DRCR.net (Diabetic
Retinopathy Clinical Research Network) trial literature. Paraphrased summary
for internal knowledge-base use only.

## Panretinal Photocoagulation (PRP)
  - Primary treatment for high-risk PDR and often considered for severe
    NPDR / non-high-risk PDR in patients with poor follow-up reliability.
  - Works by ablating peripheral ischemic retina, reducing the angiogenic
    (VEGF) drive behind neovascularization.
  - Can cause peripheral visual field constriction and may transiently
    worsen macular edema -- clinicians should evaluate/treat coexisting DME
    before or alongside PRP when feasible.

## Anti-VEGF Therapy (e.g. ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab)
  - First-line for center-involving diabetic macular edema.
  - Also increasingly used as an alternative or adjunct to PRP for PDR,
    particularly when foveal-threatening DME coexists.
  - Requires repeated intravitreal injections (monthly initially, then
    treat-and-extend or PRN regimens); durability and injection burden are
    key patient-communication points.
  - DRCR.net trials (e.g. Protocol S) found anti-VEGF monotherapy for PDR
    can achieve visual outcomes comparable to PRP, with less peripheral
    field loss, but requires reliable follow-up -- an important referral/
    treatment-planning consideration for patients with limited access to
    care.
  - The RISE and RIDE trials (ranibizumab for DME with CST >= 275
    micrometers on time-domain OCT and corresponding vision loss) showed
    improvement in macular edema within about a week of the first
    injection, with a meaningful proportion of patients gaining >= 15
    ETDRS letters (3 lines) of visual acuity with sustained monthly
    dosing; rescue focal laser was permitted from month 3 onward in
    non-responders.
  - As of the early-to-mid 2020s, an estimated ~90% of US retina
    specialists reported using anti-VEGF as their primary treatment
    for DME-related vision loss, reflecting a broader shift away from
    laser-first management for center-involving DME.

## Focal/Grid Laser
  - Historically used for non-center-involving DME; now often reserved as
    an adjunct or for patients who cannot tolerate ongoing injections.
  - The original ETDRS protocol recommended re-examining DME patients
    every 4-6 months and treating with focal argon laser once CSME
    criteria were met -- this remained standard of care until anti-VEGF
    agents became widely available in the early-to-mid 2010s and is still
    relevant for non-center-involving disease or resource-limited
    settings without ready anti-VEGF access.

## Corticosteroids (intravitreal implants)
  - Considered for DME refractory to anti-VEGF or in pseudophakic patients;
    carries elevated risk of cataract progression and intraocular pressure
    rise, requiring IOP monitoring.

## Vitrectomy
  - Indicated for non-clearing vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal
    detachment involving or threatening the macula, or combined
    tractional-rhegmatogenous detachment.

## Treatment selection considerations for a decision-support agent
  - Severity grade alone is insufficient -- DME status, prior treatment
    history, patient adherence/follow-up reliability, and systemic factors
    (glycemic control, blood pressure, pregnancy, renal status) all modify
    the appropriate treatment pathway.
  - Any automated system should present treatment options as
    recommendations for physician review, not as autonomous prescribing
    decisions -- final treatment selection is a clinical judgment call by
    a licensed ophthalmologist.
